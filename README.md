# Retention incentive evaluation

A small, reproducible impact evaluation demo in two parts.

1. **Can observational methods recover a known causal effect?** On the public LaLonde / NSW
   job training data, five estimators are compared against the answer from a randomised
   experiment, with bootstrap intervals, balance tables and overlap plots.
2. **How would I evaluate a retention bonus?** A pre-analysis plan for a hypothetical,
   randomised retention bonus pilot for a critical staff group: randomisation, power, primary
   outcome, follow-up, risks of side effects and a decision rule. The planned analysis
   (including a Kaplan-Meier and Cox survival view) is run on **simulated data** to show that
   it works.

A one page decision brief in Danish is generated from the same run
([brief/beslutningsoplaeg.md](brief/beslutningsoplaeg.md), also as HTML). It separates
*Hvad data viser*, *Sandsynlige forklaringer* and *Hvad vi endnu ikke har belæg for*, and
states expected effect, resource need, feasibility and risk.

## What is real and what is simulated

| Part | Data | Status |
|---|---|---|
| `causal/` | NSW experimental sample (185 treated, 260 randomised controls), PSID (2,490) and CPS (15,992) survey controls, Dehejia and Wahba (1999) version | **Real**, public, 1970s US data |
| `design/` | staff, units, exits, bonus amount, effect size | **Simulated** or assumed; labelled in every file |
| `brief/` | section 1 uses the real NSW results; everything about a bonus pilot is an assumption or simulated | mixed, labelled in the brief |

No Forsvaret data, no Danish register data and no real experiment were used.

Bias intervals (joint bootstrap of estimate minus benchmark) that exclude zero: PSID Difference in means, PSID OLS with covariates, CPS Difference in means. A propensity score specification change moves an estimate by up to 2 000 USD. PSID weighting fails its balance check after weighting (max abs SMD 0.34), rests on about 10 effective controls, and gives -538 USD once trimmed to scores between 0.1 and 0.9.

**Simulated pilot (not real data).** Baseline retention assumed 80%. Required n for +5 points at 80% power: 1812 (plan uses 1900; simulated power 85.2%, Monte Carlo SE 1.8, 400 pilots). Randomising the 12 units instead: minimum detectable effect 10.3 points at an assumed ICC of 0.02, 7.1 at 0.005.

One simulated pilot: primary effect 4.0 points (95% CI 0.6 to 7.4) against a true 5; extra exits in months 13 to 15 3.4 points (1.3 to 5.5), so 85% of the gain is given back; gap at 24 months 0.8 points (-3.3 to 5.0). The harm test detects the built-in spike in 96% of simulated pilots and rejects in 5% of pilots without one.

### What the NSW comparison shows

* A naive comparison with survey controls gets the sign wrong. The people who joined the
  programme were very different from the average PSID or CPS respondent before it started.
* Adjusting for prior earnings and demographics moves most estimates towards the benchmark,
  plausibly because selection into NSW ran mainly through observed prior earnings and
  unemployment (Dehejia and Wahba 1999). That reading is contested: Smith and Todd (2005)
  show it is sensitive to the Dehejia and Wahba subsample. This run does not test it.
* On PSID the adjusted estimates are not trustworthy even where they look close. OLS
  extrapolates over controls with no counterpart among the treated, matching swings with the
  propensity score specification, and weighting fails its own balance check, rests on a
  handful of heavily weighted controls and changes sign when the sample is trimmed to
  comparable people. CPS, with better overlap, is more stable.
* The bias intervals are wider than the effect itself, so "the interval includes the
  benchmark" is weak evidence. Without the experiment there would be no way to tell which
  adjusted estimate to trust. That is the argument for randomising a retention bonus instead
  of comparing those who stay with those who leave.

### What the simulated pilot shows

* Under the planning assumptions, the pilot needs individual randomisation within strata.
  Randomising whole units would need a far larger effect to be detectable with the same
  number of people, and how much larger depends on an ICC that has to be estimated from real
  HR records first (sensitivity table in the plan).
* A bonus paid at month 12 can postpone exits rather than prevent them. The simulation builds
  in such a spike plus unobserved differences between people. The pre-specified harm outcome
  is measured on everyone randomised, so it stays a randomised comparison; its false alarm
  rate and power are estimated by simulation. A single pooled hazard ratio and the global
  proportional hazards test miss the timing effect.
* The plan fixes a decision rule in advance (effect above zero, at most half the gain given
  back after payment, cost per extra month of service below the cost of an unfilled post).
* The simulated numbers only show that the analysis recovers effects that are known by
  construction. They say nothing about how a real bonus would work.

## How to run

Requirements: [uv](https://docs.astral.sh/uv/), Python 3.11 or later, R 4.x with `jsonlite`
(`foreign` and `survival` ship with R). The full run takes about 2 minutes on an 8 GB M1.

```bash
make all      # causal estimates, simulated pilot, R cross-check, documents
make check    # ruff, pytest, documents match the run outputs
make site     # static site in site/
```

The three raw data files are cached in `data/raw/` and verified against SHA-256 digests in
`src/rie/data.py`; if one is missing it is downloaded from
<https://users.nber.org/~rdehejia/data/> and checked.

Layout:

| Path | Content |
|---|---|
| `src/rie/data.py` | download, checksum, sample construction |
| `src/rie/estimators.py` | difference in means, OLS, propensity score matching, IPW, AIPW (all for the ATT), joint bootstrap, balance, trimming |
| `src/rie/causal.py` | runs every estimator on every sample, writes `causal/` |
| `src/rie/pilot.py` | power and MDE, stratified randomisation, simulation, planned analysis, survival, operating characteristics |
| `src/rie/report.py` | generates every document that contains numbers; `--check` fails if one is stale |
| `r/crosscheck.R` | benchmark, Welch CI, IPW (PSID and CPS) and the simulated Cox numbers, recomputed in R from the raw files |
| `tests/` | offline tests on the cached data and on simulations with known answers |

## Limitations

* NSW is one programme, in one country, fifty years ago, with 185 treated people. That some
  adjusted estimates land near the benchmark here does not show they would elsewhere, and
  the result depends on the Dehejia and Wahba subsample (Smith and Todd 2005).
* Matching has no caliper or bias correction, and its bootstrap interval is not
  theoretically valid (Abadie and Imbens 2008); it is shown for comparison only.
* One propensity score specification is used for both samples; Dehejia and Wahba tuned
  theirs per sample, so the observational numbers differ from theirs.
* Every pilot parameter (baseline retention, effect size, ICC, bonus size, frailty, the
  post payment spike) is an assumption. The replacement cost of a leaver, which decides
  whether a bonus pays off, is not estimated at all.
* No real personnel data, no Danish register data and no real experiment.

## Review

An independent review (a separate AI session with no access to the build conversation)
checked the estimators, the power formulas, the claims against the outputs, the Danish
brief, the tests and the workflows. Its findings were fixed in the commit after the first
one, among them: a budget figure in the brief that did not add up, a harm check that
conditioned on staying to month 12, bias intervals that treated the benchmark as known,
inconsistent SMD denominators, an ICC assumption the simulation contradicted, and an
unhedged causal explanation.

## Use of AI

The code, tests, documents and the Danish brief were drafted with Claude Code (an AI coding
assistant) to a specification I wrote. Checks that do not depend on trusting that output:
the experimental benchmark reproduces the published 1,794 USD; an independent R
implementation reproduces the benchmark, IPW and Cox numbers; tests on simulated data with
known effects; every number in the documents is generated from the run, with CI failing if
a document drifts from the outputs; and the independent review above.
